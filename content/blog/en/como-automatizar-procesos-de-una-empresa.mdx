The word "automation" sounds like a big project, months of development, and an investment only large companies can afford. In practice, most automations that actually move the needle are small: a flow that today takes 20 manual minutes and can solve itself.

## Start with what you already do every day

Before automating, identify the tasks your team repeats without thinking: copying data from one system to another, sending the same email with minor variations, building the same report every week. Those are the ideal candidates, for three reasons:

- They're repetitive and predictable — they don't require human judgment in each case.
- They take up time that people could spend on something else.
- The cost of an error (which already happens, just manually) goes down when the process is consistent.

## Three questions before automating anything

1. **Does this process repeat at least once a week?** If it's something that happens once a year, it's probably not worth automating yet.
2. **Are the rules of the process clear?** If every case "depends," automating it right away can create more problems than solutions — you need to simplify the rules first.
3. **What happens if it fails?** Every automation needs a way to detect when something went wrong, not just to run when everything goes right.

## Don't automate a broken process

Automating a badly designed process just makes errors happen faster. That's why at DTA we don't start by coding: we first understand the real process, simplify it if needed, and then automate it. It's the same logic behind our [Automation & AI](/en/soluciones/inteligencia-artificial) solution: it's not about adding artificial intelligence because it's trendy, but about solving a specific bottleneck.

## A simple example

A services company received quote requests via WhatsApp, email, and a web form. Each request was manually copied into a spreadsheet, and follow-up depended on someone remembering to message the client. The result: quotes that took days to go out, and prospects who cooled off.

The automation wasn't complex: the three sources were centralized in one place, the quote was generated from a template, and automatic follow-up reminders were scheduled. Response time dropped from days to hours, without hiring anyone new.
